# Attention is not Explanation

**Authors:** Sarthak Jain (Northeastern University), Byron C. Wallace (Northeastern University)

**Venue/Journal:** arXiv preprint, arXiv:1902.10186v3 [cs.CL], 8 May 2019. Published at NAACL 2019.

**arXiv:** [https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10186](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10186)

---

## Abstract

Attention mechanisms have seen wide adoption in neural NLP models. In addition to improving predictive performance, these are often touted as affording transparency: models equipped with attention provide a distribution over attended-to input units, and this is often presented (at least implicitly) as communicating the relative importance of inputs. However, it is unclear what relationship exists between attention weights and model outputs. In this work the authors perform extensive experiments across a variety of NLP tasks that aim to assess the degree to which attention weights provide meaningful "explanations" for predictions. They find that they largely do not. Learned attention weights are frequently uncorrelated with gradient-based measures of feature importance, and one can identify very different attention distributions that nonetheless yield equivalent predictions. The findings show that standard attention modules do not provide meaningful explanations and should not be treated as though they do.

---

## Problem Statement

Attention mechanisms are commonly claimed to provide interpretability and transparency in neural NLP models -- the assumption being that inputs (e.g., words) with high attention weights are "responsible for" model outputs. This paper formally evaluates whether this assumption holds, testing whether attention weights truly constitute faithful explanations for model predictions.

---

## Key Contributions

1. Demonstrated that learned attention weights are frequently uncorrelated with gradient-based and leave-one-out (LOO) feature importance measures across multiple NLP tasks.
2. Showed that adversarial attention distributions -- which are maximally different from the original -- can yield effectively equivalent model predictions, undermining the claim that attention explains model behavior.
3. Showed that randomly permuting attention weights often induces only minimal changes in output for BiLSTM-based models.
4. Contrasted BiLSTM (recurrent) encoders with simple feedforward (averaging) encoders, finding that attention weights in simple encoders exhibit much better correspondence with feature importance measures.
5. Proposed a formal optimization-based framework for finding adversarial attention distributions and a metric (epsilon-max JSD) for quantifying the reliability of attention as explanation.
6. Released code for reproducibility at [https://github.com/successar/AttentionExplanation](https://github.com/successar/AttentionExplanation).

## Methodology/Architecture

### Model Architecture
The authors use a standard attentive BiLSTM architecture across all tasks:
- **Embedding layer:** Pre-trained word embeddings (FastText or GloVe) produce dense token representations.
- **Encoder:** Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) producing hidden states. For contrast, a simple feedforward "average" encoder (linear projection + ReLU) and ConvNet encoders are also examined.
- **Attention mechanism:** Softmax over similarity scores between hidden states and a query vector. Two similarity functions are tested: Additive (Bahdanau-style) and Scaled Dot-Product (Vaswani-style).
- **Decoder:** A dense layer consumes the weighted context vector to produce predictions.

### Experimental Framework

**Experiment 1 -- Correlation with Feature Importance (Section 4.1):**
- Compute Kendall tau correlations between attention weights and (a) gradient-based feature importance scores, and (b) leave-one-out (LOO) importance scores.
- Gradients are computed with the attention distribution held fixed (computation graph disconnected at the attention module).

**Experiment 2 -- Counterfactual Attention (Section 4.2):**
- **Attention Permutation (4.2.1):** Randomly permute attention weights 100 times and measure median change in output (Total Variation Distance).
- **Adversarial Attention (4.2.2):** Solve a constrained optimization problem to find attention distributions that are maximally different (measured by Jensen-Shannon Divergence) from the original while keeping the prediction within epsilon of the original output. The objective maximizes JSD while constraining TVD of output to be within epsilon (0.01 for classification, 0.05 for QA).

### Metrics
- **Kendall tau** for correlation between attention and feature importance.
- **Total Variation Distance (TVD)** for change in output distributions.
- **Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD)** for distance between attention distributions.

## Results

### Correlation with Feature Importance
- For BiLSTM encoders, Kendall tau correlations between attention and gradients are **modest** (typically 0.1--0.5), with many instances showing weak or no significant correlation.
- For simple "average" (feedforward) encoders, gradient correlations with attention are **substantially higher** (typically 0.5--0.8).
- LOO and gradient measures correlate with each other much more strongly than either does with attention weights.

### Attention Permutation
- Many instances show small median output differences (delta-y^med) even when max attention weights are large, meaning scrambling attention often has little effect on predictions.
- Exception: For the MIMIC Diabetes task (positive class), perturbing attention causes large changes, likely because a few high-precision tokens indicate the positive class.

### Adversarial Attention
- Across most datasets, it is frequently possible to find adversarial attention distributions with near-maximal JSD (approaching the upper bound of 0.69) that yield predictions within epsilon of the original.
- This means very different "explanations" (attention heatmaps) can produce identical outputs.
- The relationship between high max attention and difficulty finding adversarial distributions exists but is weak.

---

## Limitations

- The authors acknowledge that Kendall tau may be depressed by noise from irrelevant features, though they argue this does not fully explain the weak correlations.
- Only a handful of attention variants are considered (Additive and Scaled Dot-Product); alternative attention specifications may yield different conclusions.
- Only RNN-based (BiLSTM) and simple feedforward encoders are analyzed in depth; Transformer-based self-attention architectures are not directly studied.
- The counterfactual adversarial attention weights may be scored as unlikely under the attention module parameters -- they represent possible but not necessarily probable alternative explanations.
- Only tasks with unstructured output spaces are evaluated; seq2seq tasks (e.g., translation) are left for future work.
- Whether agreement between attention and feature importance is strong for only the top-k features remains unexplored.

## Relevance to CTRA

This paper is relevant to the CTRA project in several ways:

1. **Interpretability of NLP models for clinical trial risk assessment:** If the CTRA project uses attention-based models (e.g., BERT, BiLSTM with attention) to predict protocol uninformativeness, attention weights should NOT be naively interpreted as explanations for why a trial is flagged as uninformative.
2. **Model transparency requirements:** For clinical trial risk assessment to be actionable, stakeholders need to understand why a protocol is flagged. This paper cautions against relying on attention heatmaps for that purpose and motivates the use of more rigorous explainability methods (e.g., gradient-based methods, SHAP, LIME, or inherently interpretable models).
3. **Choice of encoder architecture matters:** The finding that simple feedforward encoders produce more interpretable attention weights could inform architectural decisions for the CTRA system if interpretability is a priority.
4. **Clinical NLP context:** The paper uses MIMIC clinical datasets (Diabetes, Anemia ICD9 coding from discharge summaries), demonstrating the relevance of these findings to healthcare NLP tasks similar to those in CTRA.
