# Harms From Uninformative Clinical Trials

**Authors:**
- Deborah A. Zarin, MD (Multi-Regional Clinical Trials Center, Harvard Medical School, Cambridge, Massachusetts)
- Steven N. Goodman, MD, MHS, PhD (Stanford University School of Medicine, Stanford, California)
- Jonathan Kimmelman, PhD (Biomedical Ethics Unit, McGill University, Montreal, Quebec, Canada)

**Venue/Journal:** JAMA (Journal of the American Medical Association), Viewpoint, Volume 322, Number 9, September 3, 2019, pp. 813-814.

**DOI:** [10.1001/jama.2019.9892](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.9892)

**Published Online:** July 25, 2019

---

## Abstract

Individuals who enroll in clinical trials do so with the belief that their participation will help advance medical science. However, many trials are designed, conducted, and reported in ways that stymie this objective -- a problem that can be called "uninformativeness." From the perspective of researchers, this is a form of research inefficiency. But from the perspective of participants, preventable uninformativeness is a serious breach of trust and a violation of research ethics. The authors define what makes a trial uninformative, identify why uninformative trials persist despite layers of oversight, and propose four broad recommendations for reform.

---

## Problem Statement

Many clinical trials fail to provide results that are of meaningful use for patients, clinicians, researchers, or policy makers. These "uninformative" trials violate the social contract between researchers and participants, squander scarce resources, divert participants from better studies, and dilute the evidence base of medicine. Despite existing funding, review, and reporting processes, uninformative trials remain highly prevalent. The current research system has strong incentives to conduct more trials but few counterincentives for uninformative ones, and no single oversight mechanism assesses all necessary conditions for informativeness.

## Key Contributions

1. **Defined five necessary conditions** for a clinical trial to be informative:
   - (1) The study hypothesis addresses an important and unresolved scientific, medical, or policy question.
   - (2) The study is designed to provide meaningful evidence related to this question.
   - (3) The study is demonstrably feasible (e.g., has a realistic plan for recruiting sufficient participants).
   - (4) The study is conducted and analyzed in a scientifically valid manner.
   - (5) The study reports methods and results accurately, completely, and promptly.

2. **Identified the ethical dimensions** of uninformative trials beyond mere research inefficiency -- framing the issue as a breach of trust and a violation of the principle of social value that justifies asking people to participate in research.

3. **Documented the scale of the problem** with concrete examples:
   - A 2013 systematic review of otitis media had to exclude 24 of 96 eligible trials due to excess risk of bias.
   - 67 of the largest unreported trials on ClinicalTrials.gov had no results reported in the public domain a median of 9 years after completion, affecting >87,000 participants.
   - As of March 2019, 9,484 open trials enrolling >5 million US participants had no evidence of external funding or independent scientific review.

4. **Diagnosed systemic causes:** Strong incentives to conduct research combined with inadequate research training; no oversight mechanism assesses all five elements; ethics committees do not typically assess scientific merit; funders do not review full protocols; quality of reporting cannot be addressed prior to study initiation.

5. **Proposed four broad recommendations:**
   - (1) Academic medical centers (AMCs) and research sponsors should ensure meaningful independent scientific review prior to trial initiation, focusing on trials at greatest risk (e.g., those without external funding, by less experienced investigators, with high-risk-of-bias designs).
   - (2) Develop metrics reflecting the extent to which the five conditions of informative trials are being met.
   - (3) Create incentives rewarding informative trials and discouraging uninformative ones.
   - (4) Funders should require more detailed protocol review and hold researchers accountable for scientifically appropriate and timely conduct, analysis, and reporting.

## Methodology/Architecture

This is a **Viewpoint/Opinion piece**, not an empirical research study. The authors synthesize existing evidence and scholarship on clinical trial quality, drawing on:
- Published systematic reviews documenting exclusion of biased trials
- Data from ClinicalTrials.gov on unreported and unfunded trials
- The FDAAA (Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act 2007) TrialsTracker website
- Existing ethical frameworks (CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines for Health-Related Research)
- Prior scholarship by Altman (1994), Freedman (1987), and others on research waste and ethical requirements

## Limitations

- As a viewpoint/opinion piece, this paper does not present original empirical research or quantitative analysis.
- The proposed recommendations are high-level and the authors acknowledge that solutions will not be simple, must be evidence-based, and must avoid deterring informative research.
- The paper does not provide a formal operational definition or scoring system for "uninformativeness" that could be directly implemented as a computational metric.
- The focus is primarily on the US research system (AMCs, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDAAA), though the issues are global.

## Relevance to CTRA

This paper is **foundational** to the CTRA project -- it is arguably the conceptual motivation for the entire project:

1. **Defines the core problem CTRA aims to address:** The CTRA project predicts protocol uninformativeness, and this paper provides the authoritative definition of what makes a clinical trial uninformative (the five necessary conditions). These conditions could serve as the conceptual framework for CTRA's labeling scheme and feature engineering.

2. **Directly motivates automated screening:** The authors call for mechanisms to identify uninformative trials early in their lifecycle and for metrics reflecting informativeness. CTRA is precisely such a mechanism -- an automated, early-stage risk assessment tool.

3. **Identifies specific risk factors:** The paper identifies concrete signals of uninformativeness that CTRA could operationalize as features:
   - Lack of external funding
   - Inexperienced investigators
   - Design characteristics carrying high risk of bias
   - Absence of independent scientific review
   - Poor past reporting track records

4. **Establishes the ethical imperative:** The paper frames uninformative trials not merely as inefficient but as ethically harmful -- participants contribute their bodies and time expecting to advance science, and uninformative trials betray that trust. This strengthens the case for developing CTRA.

5. **Quantifies the scale:** With >9,000 open trials enrolling >5 million participants potentially lacking adequate scientific review, the potential impact of an automated screening tool like CTRA is enormous.

6. **Calls for metrics development:** The explicit recommendation to develop metrics for informativeness directly aligns with CTRA's mission to create a computational operationalization of protocol risk.

7. **The five conditions map to CTRA prediction targets:**
   - Condition 1 (important question) -- addressable via literature/registry analysis
   - Condition 2 (adequate design) -- addressable via protocol feature extraction
   - Condition 3 (feasibility) -- addressable via enrollment prediction models
   - Condition 4 (valid conduct) -- partly addressable via protocol risk features
   - Condition 5 (complete reporting) -- addressable via reporting compliance data
