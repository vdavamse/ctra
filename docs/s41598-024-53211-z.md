## Abstract

The past decade has seen substantial innovation in clinical trials, including new trial formats, endpoints, and others. Also there have been regulatory changes, increasing competitive pressures and other external factors which impact clinical trials. In parallel, trial timelines have increased and success rates remain stubbornly low. This has led many observers to question whether clinical trials have become overly complex and if this complexity is always needed. Here we present a large-scale analysis of protocols and other data from over 16,000 trials. Using a machine learning algorithm, we automatically assessed key features of these trials, such as number of endpoints, number of inclusion-exclusion criteria and others. Using a regression analysis we combined these features into a new metric, the Trial Complexity Score, which correlates with overall clinical trial duration. Looking at this score across different clinical phases and therapeutic areas we see substantial increases over time, suggesting that clinical trials are indeed becoming more complex. We discuss drivers of increasing trial complexity, necessary or helpful ('good') complexity versus unnecessary ('bad') complexity, and we explore mechanisms of how sponsors of clinical trials can reduce trial complexity where appropriate.

## Problem Statement

Clinical trials have become increasingly complex over the past decade, driven by new trial formats, novel endpoints, new biomarkers, broader data sources, new regulatory guidelines, and competitive pressures. Trial timelines have increased and success rates remain stubbornly low. The paper addresses the question: Are clinical trials indeed becoming more complex, and can this complexity be quantified and correlated with trial outcomes such as duration?

## Key Contributions

1. Developed the **Trial Complexity Score**, a single composite metric (0--100%) that quantifies clinical trial complexity based on multiple protocol features.
2. Demonstrated that the score correlates with overall trial duration: a **10 percentage point increase** in Trial Complexity Score correlates with approximately **one-third longer trial duration**.
3. Showed that clinical trial complexity has **increased by more than 10 percentage points** over the last 10 years across all phases (from low-30s to mid-40s).
4. Provided phase-specific analysis: Phase 1 trials increased from low-20s to mid-30s; Phase 2 and Phase 3 increased from mid-40s to low-to-mid 50s, confirming that "Phase 1 is the new Phase 3."
5. Analyzed complexity trends across five major therapy areas (oncology, immunology, CNS/neurology, cardiovascular, endocrinology), identifying distinct patterns and drivers for each.
6. Distinguished between "good" complexity (necessary for rigorous science, regulatory compliance, unmet medical need) and "bad" complexity (unnecessary duplication of endpoints, excessive exploratory endpoints).

## Methodology/Architecture

**Data extraction:**
- Primary source: ClinicalTrials.gov AACT database, augmented with versioned data from ClinicalTrials.gov web interface
- Features extracted directly from AACT: therapeutic area, number of patients enrolled, number of eligibility criteria, number of endpoints, number of arms
- Features extracted via fuzzy matching: number of sites, number of countries

**Data filtering pipeline:**
1. All industry-sponsored interventional trials since 2010 (~64,000 trials)
2. Filtered to completed trials (~38,000)
3. Removed trials with duration < 1 month (~36,000)
4. Removed outliers (features > 5 standard deviations from mean; ~35,000)
5. Focused on top 100 sponsors by trial volume: **final dataset of 16,790 trials**

**Feature engineering:**
- **Baseline features:** Therapeutic area and trial phase (one-hot encoded). Phase groups: Phase I, Phase I/II and II, Phase II/III and III. Therapy areas: Oncology, Immunology, CNS, Cardiovascular and Metabolic, Respiratory, Musculoskeletal, Infectious diseases.
- **Design features:** Number of sites, patient enrollment, number of countries, number of endpoints, number of eligibility criteria, number of arms -- each converted to percentile ranking.
- **Target variable:** Logarithm of trial duration (months between first protocol version and primary completion date).

**Regression analysis:**
- Combined a **Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) Regressor** (for non-linear relationships) with an **OLS Ridge model** (for explainability)
- Model performance:
  - Phase 1: R^2 = 44.2%, MAPE = 0.637
  - Phase 2: R^2 = 28.2%, MAPE = 0.387
  - Phase 3: R^2 = 37.2%, MAPE = 0.414

**Feature importance ranking:** Therapeutic area > number of sites > number of patients enrolled > number of countries > number of eligibility criteria > number of endpoints > number of arms.

## Results

**Overall trends:**
- Average complexity score increased by >10 percentage points over 10 years (low-30s to mid-40s)
- All phases show increases; Phase 1 increase is most pronounced (both relative and absolute)

**By therapy area:**
- **Oncology:** Historically most complex; steady increase 2014--2020, leveled off since (possibly COVID-19 impact). Major indications: prostate, colorectal, breast, lung cancer.
- **Immunology:** Average complexity. Crohn's disease, MS, and stroke contribute to higher complexity. Spikes driven by changes in sponsors and modalities.
- **CNS/Neurology:** Average complexity historically.
- **Cardiovascular:** Large increases up to 2021, driven by wearable device endpoint adoption.
- **Endocrinology:** Historically lowest but steadily rising, driven by shift from diabetes to NASH and digital endpoints.

**Key heuristic:** A 10 percentage point increase in Trial Complexity Score correlates with ~33% longer trial duration. This implies trial durations can vary 3--5x even within the same indication.

**Complexity variability:** Within every disease area, complexity scores span a very wide range (e.g., oncology from 20s to 90s), indicating complexity is not inevitable and can be managed through trial design choices.

## Limitations

- The study considers only features available in public databases; important complexity drivers like site characteristics, data types (wearables, OMICs), visit frequency, DMC use, and interim analyses could not be included.
- Correlation with duration does not imply causation.
- Only industry-sponsored trials from the top 100 sponsors are included, which may not represent all clinical trial activity.
- The MAPE values (0.387--0.637) indicate substantial residual variance not explained by the model.
- COVID-19 may have distorted recent trends.
- The study does not directly measure trial success/failure rates in relation to complexity.
- Authors are employees of BCG, a management consultancy serving biopharma companies, which is a potential conflict of interest.

## Relevance to CTRA

This paper is relevant to CTRA in the following ways:

1. **Quantifies protocol complexity features** that are directly related to CTRA's goal of predicting trial uninformativeness. The features used (number of endpoints, eligibility criteria, arms, sites, countries, enrollment) overlap substantially with features CTRA may extract from protocols.
2. **Demonstrates that protocol design features predict trial outcomes** (duration), supporting CTRA's hypothesis that protocol-level features can predict operational risk.
3. **Provides a methodology template** for combining multiple protocol features into a single predictive score, which parallels CTRA's approach of combining features to predict uninformativeness.
4. **Establishes that increasing complexity correlates with longer timelines**, which is one dimension of operational risk. Excessively complex or poorly designed protocols may also be more likely to be uninformative.
5. **Uses the same primary data source** (ClinicalTrials.gov) that CTRA relies on, and the NLP-based feature extraction from protocol text aligns with CTRA's approach.
6. **Identifies "bad" complexity** (unnecessary duplication of endpoints, excessive exploratory endpoints) that may contribute to uninformative trials -- a direct link to CTRA's prediction target.
7. **Feature importance findings** (eligibility criteria and endpoints as complexity drivers) can inform which protocol features CTRA should prioritize.
