## Abstract

Success rate of clinical trials (CTs) is low, with the protocol design itself being considered a major risk factor. The authors investigate the use of deep learning methods to predict the risk of CTs based on their protocols. Considering protocol changes and their final status, a retrospective risk assignment method is proposed to label CTs according to low, medium, and high risk levels. Transformer and graph neural networks are designed and combined in an ensemble model to learn to infer the ternary risk categories. The ensemble model achieved robust performance (AUROC of 0.8453 [95% CI: 0.8409--0.8495]), similar to the individual architectures but significantly outperforming a baseline based on bag-of-words features (0.7548 [0.7493--0.7603] AUROC). The study demonstrates the potential of deep learning in predicting the risk of CTs from their protocols, paving the way for customized risk mitigation strategies during protocol design.

## Problem Statement

Clinical trials have a low success rate (less than 14% from Phase I to market approval), and a significant portion of failures relate not to drug efficacy/safety but to protocol design issues such as insufficient enrollment, poor site selection, protocol amendments, and outcome changes. Existing approaches for CT risk assessment rely on binary labeling (completed vs. terminated) and hand-crafted features that do not generalize well to textual data. The paper addresses the need for (1) a finer-grained, multi-level risk label derived from historical protocol evolution, and (2) deep learning models that can automatically extract risk-predictive features from the hierarchical, semi-structured text of CT protocols.

## Key Contributions

1. **Large-scale historical analysis of CT protocol changes** -- Performed analysis of enrollment drops, study duration divergence, number of major protocol amendments, and outcome changes across statuses, phases, and clinical conditions using a web-scraped "history-set" of all protocol versions from ClinicalTrials.gov.
2. **Novel ternary risk assignment methodology** -- Proposed a new labeling scheme (low, medium, high risk) that goes beyond binary completed/terminated by incorporating five retrospective factors: final status, enrollment attrition rate, planned-vs-actual duration divergence, number of major amendments, and primary outcome changes, all stratified by phase and condition.
3. **Deep learning architecture combining transformers and GNNs** -- Designed a Sampled Language Model (transformer-based, pre-trained on PubMed) and a Graph Neural Network model that treats the CT protocol as a hierarchical graph. These are combined in an ensemble via probability averaging.
4. **Superior performance over baselines** -- The ensemble model achieved 0.8453 AUROC on ternary risk and 0.9234 AUROC on binary risk, outperforming MLP baselines and prior state-of-the-art methods (Elkin & Zhu: 0.7281 AUROC; Fu et al. HINT: 0.817 AUROC).
5. **Fine-grained benchmark dataset** -- Released a benchmark dataset with ternary risk labels for 283,776 CT-phase-condition triplets derived from 135,940 unique CT protocols, enabling future research on multi-class CT risk assessment.

## Methodology / Architecture

### Risk Label Assignment

- **Data source:** ClinicalTrials.gov snapshot (up to 2020), 360,497 protocols total, filtered to 210,497 interventional CTs with settled status (completed, terminated, unknown, withdrawn).
- **History-set:** All historical protocol versions were web-scraped (with registry administrator authorization) to track changes over time.
- **Five risk factors:** (1) Final status, (2) enrollment attrition rate (last vs. first version enrollment), (3) divergence between planned and actual study duration, (4) number of major protocol amendments (changes to Arms/Interventions, Conditions, Eligibility Criteria, Outcome Measures, Study Design, etc.), (5) primary outcome changes.
- **Stratification:** Statistics computed per phase-condition group. A CT is deemed "atypical" if a metric is >1 SD from the group mean, "very atypical" if >2 SD.
- **Decision tree for labeling:** Non-completed trials are high risk. Completed trials with very atypical enrollment drop are high risk; atypical enrollment drop leads to medium. Atypical or very atypical duration divergence leads to medium or high. Substantial amendments lead to medium. Primary outcome changes with otherwise typical metrics lead to medium. All typical metrics lead to low risk.
- **Label distribution:** 30% low, 15% medium, 55% high risk across 283,776 triplets.

### Prediction Models

1. **Sampled Language Model (Sampled-LM):** A BERT-like model (6 transformer layers, 8 attention heads, 512-token embedding) pre-trained on PubMed, then fine-tuned for CT risk classification. To handle long protocols (>512 tokens), n=8 text samples are drawn from protocol leaves weighted by token count, processed in parallel, mean-pooled, and classified. This is repeated N=100 times; the final label is the highest mean probability across repetitions.
2. **Graph Neural Network (Graph-LM / Graph-BOW):** The CT protocol is represented as a hierarchical graph following the standard CT template structure. Leaf nodes are initialized with either bag-of-words vectors (Graph-BOW) or PubMedBERT sentence embeddings (Graph-LM). Information is propagated through L=4 message-passing layers, then pooled and classified. Weighted cross-entropy loss handles class imbalance; optimized with Adam.
3. **Ensemble:** Averages the class probabilities from Sampled-LM and Graph-LM to produce the final prediction.
4. **Baselines:** MLP-BOW and MLP-LM, which concatenate features from k=15 protocol sections.

### Explainability

- Integrated gradients are used on the Graph-LM model to identify which protocol sections (Study Design, Contacts and Locations, Outcome Measures, etc.) contribute most to high-risk predictions. Analysis on breast cancer (n=28) and cardiovascular (n=36) high-risk CTs showed Study Design (32--43%) and Contacts and Locations (29--31%) as the top contributing sections.

## Limitations

- **No patient-level or drug-related information:** The study does not incorporate patient records, adverse event databases, or chemical/molecular structure of interventions. The authors note that drug-related databases (e.g., adverse event reports, chemical structures) could enrich the graph-based model as additional nodes.
- **Retrospective labeling inherently relies on post-hoc data:** The ternary risk labels are derived from historical outcomes and may not perfectly capture prospective risk.
- **Explainability is qualitative:** While integrated gradients identify risk-associated protocol sections, it is acknowledged that quantitative evaluation of explainability in healthcare is difficult.
- **Label noise in medium-risk class:** No clear boundaries between risk categories; medium risk is scattered between low and high, contributing to lower performance on that class.

## Relevance to CTRA

This paper is highly relevant to the CTRA project, which predicts protocol uninformativeness (not drug efficacy). Key connections:

- **Protocol-centric risk:** Like CTRA, this paper focuses on risk arising from the protocol design itself (enrollment issues, duration misestimation, excessive amendments, outcome changes) rather than from drug efficacy or safety. The ternary risk labeling methodology provides a richer target than simple completion/termination.
- **Feature overlap:** The risk factors used (enrollment drop, duration divergence, major amendments, outcome changes) are closely related to the concept of "uninformative" trials -- trials that undergo so many protocol changes or enrollment difficulties that they fail to produce actionable data.
- **Graph representation of protocols:** The hierarchical graph representation of CT protocols (with sections like Study Design, Eligibility Criteria, Outcome Measures as nodes) is directly applicable to CTRA's approach of analyzing structured protocol elements.
- **Explainability for design optimization:** The integrated gradients approach identifies which protocol sections contribute to high risk, which aligns with CTRA's goal of flagging protocol features that predict uninformativeness.
- **Benchmark data:** The released Zenodo dataset of 283,776 labeled triplets could serve as training data or validation reference for CTRA models.
